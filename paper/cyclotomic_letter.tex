\title{Kochen--Specker uncolorability of the cyclotomic\\ray family $S_n \subset \C^3$ occurs if and only if $6 \mid n$}

\author{Michael Kernaghan}
\affiliation{Pacific Quantum Systems, Vancouver, Canada}

\date{May 2026}

\begin{abstract}
We prove that the ray set $S_n$ generated by the $n$th roots of unity in $\C^3$ is Kochen--Specker uncolorable if and only if $6 \mid n$.  Sufficiency follows from the embedding into $S_6$ of an explicit 33-ray KS set with coordinates in $\{0\}\cup\mu_6$.  Necessity is proved by analysis of short vanishing sums of roots of unity: when $3 \mid n$ and $n$ is odd, a projective-collapse argument shows every all-nonzero triad is pairwise disjoint and the coloring constraints decompose; when $2 \mid n$ and $3 \nmid n$, the orthogonality graph on each coordinate plane is a perfect matching, admitting an explicit coloring.  The factorization $6 = 2 \times 3$ is consistent with, but logically independent of, the result of Cortez, Morales, and Reyes that $\Z[1/6]$ is the minimal ring supporting KS contextuality in dimension~3.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

\section{Introduction}

The Kochen--Specker (KS) theorem~\cite{KochenSpecker1967} establishes that quantum measurements in dimension $d \geq 3$ cannot be explained by noncontextual hidden-variable theories, by exhibiting a finite set of rays admitting no consistent $\{0,1\}$-valued coloring.  A natural source of KS constructions is the cyclotomic family: for each positive integer~$n$, the alphabet $\{0\} \cup \{\zeta^k : k = 0, \ldots, n-1\}$ with $\zeta = e^{2\pi i/n}$ generates a ray set $S_n$ in $\C^3$ via all projectively distinct nonzero vectors with coordinates drawn from this alphabet.

Cabello~\cite{Cabello2025simplest} exhibited a 33-vector, 14-basis KS set in $\C^3$ with coordinates in $\{0\}\cup\mu_6$ (hence lying in $S_6$), holding the record for fewest bases.  Cortez, Morales, and Reyes~\cite{CortezMoralesReyes2022} proved that $\Z[1/6]$ is the minimal ring extension of~$\Z$ exhibiting KS contextuality in dimension~3---i.e., the primes 2 and~3 are both necessary.

These results suggest a sharp characterization: KS-uncolorability in the root-of-unity coordinate family should require exactly $6 \mid n$.  We prove this.  (We emphasize that the relevant structure is the available root subgroup $\mu_n$, not the ambient field: $\Q(\zeta_3) = \Q(\zeta_6)$, yet $S_3$ is colorable while $S_6$ is not.)

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
The ray set $S_n$ generated by the $n$th roots of unity in $\C^3$ is KS-un\-color\-able if and only if $6 \mid n$.
\end{theorem}

\section{Preliminaries}

A \emph{ray} in $\C^3$ is a one-dimensional subspace, represented by a nonzero vector up to scalar multiples.  Two rays $v, w$ are \emph{orthogonal} if $\langle v | w \rangle = \sum_k \bar{v}_k w_k = 0$.  An orthogonal \emph{triad} is a set of three mutually orthogonal rays (an orthonormal basis up to normalization).  A \emph{KS coloring} assigns each ray a value in $\{0,1\}$ such that (I) orthogonal rays cannot both receive~1, and (II) every triad contains exactly one~1.  A ray set is \emph{KS-uncolorable} if no such coloring exists.

For $\zeta = e^{2\pi i/n}$, the ray set $S_n$ consists of all projectively distinct (modulo $\C^\times$) nonzero vectors with coordinates in $\{0\} \cup \{\zeta^j : 0 \leq j < n\}$.  We classify rays by \emph{zero pattern}: \emph{axis rays} have two zero coordinates (3~rays); \emph{one-zero rays} have exactly one zero ($3n$~rays: normalizing the first nonzero coordinate to~1 leaves one free phase, and there are 3~choices of zero position); \emph{all-nonzero rays} have no zeros ($n^2$~rays: normalizing the first coordinate to~1 leaves two free phases).

A key structural observation: in $\C^3$, the inner product $\langle v | w \rangle = \sum_k \bar{v}_k w_k$ has at most three nonzero terms.  Each nonzero term is $\overline{\zeta^a}\zeta^b = \zeta^{b-a}$, an $n$th root of unity.  Therefore only 1-, 2-, and 3-term vanishing sums of $n$th roots arise as orthogonality conditions in~$S_n$.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}}

\subsection{Sufficiency: an explicit $S_6$ witness}

If $6 \mid n$, then $\zeta^{n/6}$ generates the sixth roots of unity as a subgroup of $\langle\zeta\rangle$, so the alphabet $\{0\} \cup \mu_6$ for $S_6$ embeds coordinatewise into the alphabet for~$S_n$, and any KS-uncolorable subset of~$S_6$ remains KS-uncolorable inside~$S_n$ (any candidate coloring of $S_n$ restricts to a coloring of the subset preserving orthogonality, since orthogonality of fixed rays is intrinsic).  It therefore suffices to exhibit a single KS-uncolorable set of rays whose coordinates lie in $\{0\} \cup \mu_6$.

Cabello's 33-ray witness~\cite{Cabello2025simplest} achieves this.  Writing $\omega = \zeta^{n/3}$ for a primitive cube root of unity, the 33 rays in Table~\ref{tab:cabello33} arise as the closure of the Yu-Oh state-independent contextuality set~\cite{YuOh2012} (rays 1--13) under the projective action of the Weyl-Heisenberg generators $X : e_i \mapsto e_{(i+1) \bmod 3}$ and $Z = \mathrm{diag}(1, \omega, \omega^2)$.  Cabello proves this set is the unique 33-vector, 14-basis KS set up to unitary equivalence~\cite{Cabello2025simplest}; the rays of Table~\ref{tab:cabello33} are one such realization, with every coordinate equal to~$0$ or a sixth root of unity (since $\{\pm 1, \pm\omega, \pm\omega^2\} = \mu_6$), so the set embeds in~$S_6$.

The 14 orthogonal bases formed by these rays comprise the axis basis $\{1,2,3\}$, nine ``mixed'' bases each containing one axis ray and two one-zero rays from the corresponding coordinate plane, and four ``all-nonzero'' bases $B_{11} = \{10,20,30\}$, $B_{12} = \{11,21,31\}$, $B_{13} = \{12,22,32\}$, $B_{14} = \{13,23,33\}$, each being a Weyl-Heisenberg orbit of an $h$-ray (rays 10--13).  The full basis listing is in the supplementary material.

\begin{lemma}[Cabello~\cite{Cabello2025simplest}]\label{lem:cabello33}
The 33 rays of Table~\ref{tab:cabello33} admit no KS coloring.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof]
We give two independent verifications.  \emph{Analytic} (Cabello~\cite{Cabello2025simplest}): Yu and Oh~\cite{YuOh2012} proved that any $\{0,1\}$-coloring of rays~1--13 satisfying (I) assigns~$1$ to at most one ``$h$-ray'' (rays 10--13).  The bases $B_{11}, \ldots, B_{14}$ are the four Weyl-Heisenberg orbits of those $h$-rays, so by (II) they contribute four~$1$'s across the augmented rays.  Cabello~\cite{Cabello2025simplest} shows that this assignment is incompatible with the orthogonality edges linking the four $h$-orbits to the remaining nine bases: four mutually-orthogonal color classes require representatives among only three Yu-Oh slots.  \emph{Computational}: a SAT encoding (one variable per ray; basis clauses and pairwise no-two-$1$'s clauses) is unsatisfiable, verified with Glucose4; DRAT certificate in the supplementary material. \qedhere
\end{proof}

With the monotonicity argument above, Lemma~\ref{lem:cabello33} completes the sufficiency direction. $\square$

\subsection{Necessity: vanishing-sum analysis}

We show that if $6 \nmid n$, then $S_n$ admits a valid KS coloring.  Only sums of $\leq 3$ terms arise as orthogonality conditions in~$\C^3$ (Section~2), so we need only the elementary 2- and 3-term cases below (longer vanishing sums of roots of unity~\cite{LamLeung2000} play no role here).

\begin{lemma}[Three-term vanishing sums]\label{lem:3term}
If $u, v, w \in \langle\zeta\rangle$ and $u + v + w = 0$, then $3 \mid n$ and $\{u, v, w\} = \{\lambda, \lambda\omega, \lambda\omega^2\}$ for some $\lambda \in \langle\zeta\rangle$, where $\omega = \zeta^{n/3}$.  Conversely, $3 \mid n$ suffices.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $u = \zeta^a$, $v = \zeta^b$, $w = \zeta^c$.  If $a = b$ then $u+v+w = \zeta^a(2 + \zeta^{c-a}) = 0$ would force $|\zeta^{c-a}| = 2$, impossible; so the exponents are pairwise distinct.  Setting $x = \zeta^{b-a}$, $y = \zeta^{c-a}$ with $x, y \neq 1$, the equation becomes $1 + x + y = 0$.  Then $1 = |y|^2 = |1+x|^2 = 2 + 2\,\mathrm{Re}\,x$, so $\mathrm{Re}\,x = -1/2$, forcing $x \in \{\omega, \omega^2\}$ (and $y$ to the other).  Hence $\omega \in \langle\zeta\rangle$, requiring $3 \mid n$, and $\{u,v,w\} = \{\lambda, \lambda\omega, \lambda\omega^2\}$ with $\lambda = \zeta^a$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Two-term vanishing sums]\label{lem:2term}
The equation $\zeta^a + \zeta^b = 0$ has a solution if and only if $2 \mid n$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\zeta^a + \zeta^b = 0$ iff $\zeta^{b-a} = -1$ iff $-1 \in \langle\zeta\rangle$ iff $2 \mid n$.
\end{proof}

These lemmas, combined with the zero-pattern classification, determine all orthogonalities in~$S_n$.  For each pair of rays, the inner product $\langle v | w \rangle$ is a sum of $t$ roots of unity, where $t$ equals the number of coordinates in which both rays are nonzero:

\midrule

one-zero vs.\ one-zero (same) & 2 & $2 \mid n$ \\

\noindent{\footnotesize $^*$\emph{Necessary} divisibility condition for orthogonality between rays of the given type; the exact condition also involves phase congruences (e.g.\ all-nz vs.\ all-nz needs the exponent differences to be equilateral mod~$n$).}\smallskip

The ``diff.''\ case has $t = 1$ (the two distinct zero coordinates kill two inner-product terms; the survivor has modulus~1).  The axis rays form a triad for all~$n$.

We now treat the three cases where $6 \nmid n$.

\subsection{Case 1: $3 \nmid n$, $2 \nmid n$}

Any triad requires three mutually orthogonal rays spanning~$\C^3$.  A non-axis triad must contain at least one ray with fewer than two zero coordinates, hence at least one orthogonal pair whose inner product is a 2-term or 3-term sum of roots.  Since $3 \nmid n$ forbids 3-term vanishing sums and $2 \nmid n$ forbids 2-term vanishing sums, no such pair can be orthogonal.  The sole triad is the axis triad $\{e_1, e_2, e_3\}$, trivially colorable. $\square$

\subsection{Case 2: $3 \nmid n$, $2 \mid n$}

By Lemma~\ref{lem:3term}, no all-nonzero orthogonal pairs exist, so no triad can contain two all-nonzero rays.

\begin{lemma}[No all-nonzero ray in any triad when $3 \nmid n$]\label{lem:noallnonzero}
If $3 \nmid n$, every triad in $S_n$ contains an axis ray.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A triad not containing an axis ray consists of rays each having at most one zero coordinate.  Since no two all-nonzero rays are orthogonal ($3 \nmid n$), at most one ray is all-nonzero; the other two are one-zero.  Two one-zero rays from \emph{different} planes are never orthogonal (Lemma~\ref{lem:planes}), so both one-zero rays $r_1, r_2$ share the same zero coordinate~$k$ and lie in the plane $\{x \in \C^3 : x_k = 0\}$.  Since $r_1 \perp r_2$ are nonzero, they are linearly independent (orthogonal nonzero vectors cannot be scalar multiples), hence span the whole plane.  Any vector orthogonal to both therefore lies in its orthogonal complement, the $k$-axis: the third ray~$v$ is proportional to~$e_k$, contradicting the assumption that $v$ is all-nonzero.  A fortiori, three one-zero rays cannot form a triad (the same plane argument applies to any two, and different planes are excluded by Lemma~\ref{lem:planes}).
\end{proof}

Moreover, axis rays are never orthogonal to all-nonzero rays ($e_k \perp v$ requires $v_k = 0$).  Since every triad contains an axis ray, no all-nonzero ray appears in any triad.  Every triad has the form $\{v_1, v_2, e_k\}$ where $v_1, v_2$ are one-zero rays in the $k$th coordinate plane and $e_k$ is the complementary axis ray.

\begin{lemma}[Perfect matching]\label{lem:matching}
For even~$n$, the orthogonality graph on one-zero rays in each coordinate plane is a perfect matching.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The one-zero rays in the $z = 0$ plane are projectively $(1, \zeta^p, 0)$ for $p \in \{0, \ldots, n-1\}$.  Two such rays $p, q$ are orthogonal iff $1 + \zeta^{q-p} = 0$, i.e., $q - p \equiv n/2 \pmod{n}$.  Each ray has exactly one partner, giving $n/2$ disjoint edges.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Plane independence]\label{lem:planes}
One-zero rays from different coordinate planes are never orthogonal.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $v$ has $v_i = 0$ and $w$ has $w_j = 0$ with $i \neq j$, then $\langle v | w \rangle = \sum_k \bar{v}_k w_k$ has exactly one nonzero term (at the coordinate $k \neq i, j$), which is a product of roots of unity, hence has modulus~1. For example, $\langle(\zeta^a, \zeta^b, 0) | (\zeta^c, 0, \zeta^d)\rangle = \overline{\zeta^a}\zeta^c + 0 + 0 = \zeta^{c-a} \neq 0$.
\end{proof}

We construct an explicit coloring.  Assign $e_1 \mapsto 1$, $e_2, e_3 \mapsto 0$.  All one-zero rays in the $x{=}0$ plane receive~0 (their triads with $e_1$ already have one~1).  In each of the $y{=}0$ and $z{=}0$ planes, the matched pairs (Lemma~\ref{lem:matching}) are independently 2-colored: one~1, one~0 per pair.  All-nonzero rays receive~0.

\emph{Verification of (II)}: each triad $\{v_1, v_2, e_k\}$ has exactly one~1.  For $k = 1$: $e_1 = 1$, both partners~0.  For $k = 2, 3$: axis is~0, exactly one of the matched pair is~1.

\emph{Verification of (I)}: we check that no two rays \emph{both assigned~1} are orthogonal.  The ray $e_1$ is orthogonal only to rays with $v_1 = 0$, all of which received~0.  Any ray assigned~1 in the $y{=}0$ plane may be orthogonal to its matched partner in that plane and to one or more all-nonzero rays (via a 2-term cancellation, possible since $2 \mid n$); however the matched partner is assigned~0 by construction, $e_2$ is assigned~0, and all-nonzero rays are assigned~0.  Cross-plane one-zero rays are never orthogonal (Lemma~\ref{lem:planes}).  The same holds symmetrically for $z{=}0$.  Constraint~(I) is therefore satisfied, and the coloring is valid.  $\square$

\subsection{Case 3: $3 \mid n$, $2 \nmid n$}

This is the principal case.  Let $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3} = \zeta^{n/3}$, a primitive cube root of unity.  Three-term cancellations create triads among all-nonzero rays, but we show these triads are pairwise disjoint and the KS constraints decompose.  First, since $2 \nmid n$, no 2-term vanishing sums exist, so no all-nonzero ray is orthogonal to any one-zero ray, and no one-zero ray is orthogonal to another one-zero ray.  The all-nonzero and zero-bearing sectors are therefore completely decoupled.

\begin{lemma}[Projective collapse]\label{lem:collapse}
For any all-nonzero ray $v$ in $S_n$ with $3 \mid n$, there are exactly two projectively distinct all-nonzero rays orthogonal to~$v$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Normalize $v = (1, \zeta^p, \zeta^q)$.  For all-nonzero $w = (1, \zeta^r, \zeta^s)$,
\[
\langle v | w \rangle = 1 + \zeta^{r-p} + \zeta^{s-q}.
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:3term}, vanishing requires the three terms to form $\{\lambda, \lambda\omega, \lambda\omega^2\}$.  Since one term is~1, we need $1 \in \{\lambda, \lambda\omega, \lambda\omega^2\}$, giving $\lambda \in \{1, \omega^2, \omega\}$.  All three choices produce the same unordered set $\{1, \omega, \omega^2\}$; what differs is which element is assigned to which of the three terms $1, \zeta^{r-p}, \zeta^{s-q}$.  Since the first term is fixed at~1, only two assignments remain: $(\zeta^{r-p}, \zeta^{s-q}) = (\omega, \omega^2)$ or $(\omega^2, \omega)$.  This gives exactly two projective solutions:
\begin{align*}
w_+ &= (1,\; \omega\zeta^p,\; \omega^2\zeta^q), \\
w_- &= (1,\; \omega^2\zeta^p,\; \omega\zeta^q).
\end{align*}
(The three $\lambda$-values yield only $w_+$ and $w_-$ up to projective equivalence.)  Since $\omega \neq 1$: $w_+ \not\sim v$, $w_- \not\sim v$, and $w_+ \not\sim w_-$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Triad uniqueness and 3-cycle orbit]\label{lem:unique}
The set $\{v, w_+, w_-\}$ is a triad, and it is the unique triad among all-nonzero rays containing~$v$.  Furthermore the map $T_+: (1, \zeta^p, \zeta^q) \mapsto (1, \omega\zeta^p, \omega^2\zeta^q)$ satisfies $T_+^3 = \mathrm{id}$ with $T_+(v) = w_+$ and $T_+^2(v) = w_-$, so the $T_+$-orbit of each all-nonzero $v$ has size exactly~3 and equals its unique triad.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Orthogonality}: $\langle w_+ | w_- \rangle = 1 + \omega^2 \cdot \omega + \omega \cdot \omega^2 = 1 + \omega + \omega^2 = 0$ (using $\bar\omega = \omega^2$).  \emph{Uniqueness}: $v$ has exactly two all-nonzero orthogonal neighbors (Lemma~\ref{lem:collapse}); both lie in $\{v, w_+, w_-\}$.  \emph{Orbit size}: directly, $T_+^2(v) = (1, \omega^2\zeta^p, \omega^4\zeta^q) = (1, \omega^2\zeta^p, \omega\zeta^q) = w_-$, and $T_+^3(v) = (1, \omega^3\zeta^p, \omega^6\zeta^q) = v$.  Hence each $T_+$-orbit has exactly three elements and coincides with the triad.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[One-zero isolation]\label{lem:onezero}
For odd~$n$ with $3 \mid n$, every one-zero ray has exactly one orthogonal neighbor (the complementary axis ray) and participates in no triad.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $v$ be a one-zero ray with $v_k = 0$.  We show $v$ is orthogonal only to the axis ray~$e_k$.  Clearly $\langle v | e_k \rangle = 0$ since both nonzero coordinates of~$v$ are multiplied by~0.  For the converse, consider any other ray~$w \not\sim e_k$:

\emph{Case (a): $w$ is all-nonzero.}  Then $\langle v | w \rangle$ has exactly two nonzero terms (at the positions where $v$ is nonzero), each a root of unity.  This is a 2-term sum, which vanishes only if $2 \mid n$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:2term}).  Since $n$ is odd, $v \not\perp w$.

\emph{Case (b): $w$ is one-zero with $w_j = 0$, $j = k$ (same plane).}  Then $\langle v | w \rangle$ is again a 2-term sum of roots of unity.  Since $2 \nmid n$, $v \not\perp w$.

\emph{Case (c): $w$ is one-zero with $w_j = 0$, $j \neq k$ (different plane).}  Then exactly one coordinate position has both $v$ and $w$ nonzero, so $\langle v | w \rangle$ is a single root of unity, which cannot vanish.  Hence $v \not\perp w$ (this is Lemma~\ref{lem:planes}).

\emph{Case (d): $w$ is an axis ray $e_j$ with $j \neq k$.}  Then $\langle v | e_j \rangle = \bar{v}_j \neq 0$ since $v_j \neq 0$ (the only zero coordinate of~$v$ is at position~$k$, not~$j$).

Thus $\deg(v) = 1$ in the orthogonality graph, and $v$ cannot participate in any triad (which requires mutual orthogonality of three rays).
\end{proof}

By Lemma~\ref{lem:collapse}, every all-nonzero ray $v$ has orthogonal neighborhood exactly $N(v) = \{T_+v, T_+^2 v\}$ (two neighbors, no more); applying the same to $T_+v$ and $T_+^2v$ shows their neighborhoods are also contained in $\{v, T_+v, T_+^2v\}$, so this triple is a connected component---a $K_3$---with no edges leaving it.  The $n^2$ all-nonzero rays therefore partition into $n^2/3$ isolated triads (the $T_+$-orbits), with no orthogonality edges between distinct triads.  Since $2 \nmid n$, no all-nonzero ray is orthogonal to any one-zero ray, and no all-nonzero ray is orthogonal to any axis ray ($\langle e_k | w \rangle = \bar w_k \neq 0$ when $w$ is all-nonzero).  By Lemma~\ref{lem:onezero}, one-zero rays participate in no triad; they impose only constraint~(I) via their pendant edge to the complementary axis ray.  The all-nonzero triads, the axis triad, and the one-zero pendants are therefore mutually decoupled.

We color as follows: in each all-nonzero triad, assign one~1 and two~0's; in the axis triad, $e_1 \mapsto 1$, $e_2, e_3 \mapsto 0$; every one-zero ray gets~0.  Axiom (II) holds in each triad by construction; axiom (I) holds because no two rays assigned~$1$ are orthogonal (within-triad: by triad structure; cross-triad: no edges; axis-vs-others: $e_1$ has no all-nonzero neighbors, all one-zero rays received~0). $\square$

\emph{Smallest instances.}---For $n = 5$ (Case~1), $S_5$ has 43 rays but only the axis triad $\{e_1, e_2, e_3\}$.  For $n = 2$ (Case~2, alphabet $\{0, \pm 1\}$), one-zero rays match into orthogonal pairs (e.g.\ $(1,1,0) \perp (1,-1,0)$) and the plane coloring applies.  For $n = 3$ (Case~3), the nine all-nonzero rays form three disjoint triads---$\{(1,1,1), (1,\omega,\omega^2), (1,\omega^2,\omega)\}$ and its two $T_+$-images---each colored independently alongside the axis triad.

\section{Discussion}

Both primes are necessary for distinct reasons.  The identity $1 + \omega + \omega^2 = 0$ ($3 \mid n$) creates orthogonal triads among all-nonzero rays; the element $-1 \in \langle\zeta\rangle$ ($2 \mid n$) links them via 2-term cancellations.  Absent either, the orthogonality structure decomposes and an explicit coloring exists.  This factorization $6 = 2 \times 3$ is suggestively consistent with---though logically independent of---the result of Cortez, Morales, and Reyes~\cite{CortezMoralesReyes2022} that $\Z[1/6]$ is the minimal subring of~$\Q$ realizing KS projection matrices in $\C^3$: theirs concerns realizability of $P_v = vv^\dagger / \|v\|^2$ over subrings, ours colorability over a coordinate alphabet, and neither implies the other.  Whether the projective-collapse mechanism of Lemma~\ref{lem:collapse} extends to other algebraic alphabets (Gaussian integers, quadratic integer rings) is open.

\textit{Formal verification.}---The necessity argument and the sufficiency reduction $S_6 \subseteq S_n$ are machine-checked in Lean~4~\cite{Lean4} against Mathlib~v4.30; the KS-uncolorability of the 33-ray witness (Table~\ref{tab:cabello33}) is independently verified by SAT (Glucose4), with certificate in the supplementary material.
