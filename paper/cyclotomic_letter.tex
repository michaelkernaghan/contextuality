\title{Kochen--Specker uncolorability in root-of-unity\\coordinate alphabets requires exactly $6 \mid n$}

\author{Michael Kernaghan}
\affiliation{Pacific Quantum Systems, Vancouver, Canada}

\date{March 2026}

\begin{abstract}
We prove that the ray set $S_n$ generated by the $n$th roots of unity in $\C^3$ is Kochen--Specker uncolorable if and only if $6 \mid n$.  Sufficiency follows from the embedding of Cabello's Eisenstein 33-vector KS set.  Necessity is proved by algebraic analysis of vanishing sums among roots of unity: when $n$ is odd and $3 \mid n$, a projective collapse argument shows every triad is pairwise disjoint (no two share a ray) and the KS coloring constraints decompose into independent subproblems; when $3 \nmid n$ and $2 \mid n$, the orthogonality graph on each coordinate plane is a perfect matching, admitting an explicit coloring.  The factorization $6 = 2 \times 3$ mirrors the ring-theoretic result of Cortez, Morales, and Reyes that $\Z[1/6]$ is the minimal ring supporting KS contextuality in dimension~3, though the two results are logically independent: ours concerns colorability of a coordinate-alphabet family, theirs concerns realizability of projectors over subrings of~$\Q$.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

\section{Introduction}

The Kochen--Specker (KS) theorem~\cite{KochenSpecker1967} establishes that quantum measurements in dimension $d \geq 3$ cannot be explained by noncontextual hidden-variable theories, by exhibiting a finite set of rays admitting no consistent $\{0,1\}$-valued coloring.  A natural source of KS constructions is the cyclotomic family: for each positive integer~$n$, the alphabet $\{0\} \cup \{\zeta^k : k = 0, \ldots, n-1\}$ with $\zeta = e^{2\pi i/n}$ generates a ray set $S_n$ in $\C^3$ via all projectively distinct nonzero vectors with coordinates drawn from this alphabet.

Cabello~\cite{Cabello2025simplest} (preprint) showed that $S_6$ contains a 33-vector KS set built from the Eisenstein integers $\Z[\omega]$ ($\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$), which he identified as the ``simplest'' KS set by the criterion of fewest bases.  Cortez, Morales, and Reyes~\cite{CortezMoralesReyes2022} proved that $\Z[1/6]$ is the minimal ring extension of~$\Z$ exhibiting KS contextuality in dimension~3---that is, the primes 2 and~3 are both necessary.

These results suggest a sharp characterization: KS-uncolorability in the root-of-unity coordinate family should require exactly $6 \mid n$.  We prove this.  (We emphasize that the relevant structure is the available root subgroup $\mu_n$, not the ambient field: $\Q(\zeta_3) = \Q(\zeta_6)$, yet $S_3$ is colorable while $S_6$ is not.)

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
The ray set $S_n$ generated by the $n$th roots of unity in $\C^3$ is KS-un\-color\-able if and only if $6 \mid n$.
\end{theorem}

\section{Preliminaries}

A \emph{ray} in $\C^3$ is a one-dimensional subspace, represented by a nonzero vector up to scalar multiples.  Two rays $v, w$ are \emph{orthogonal} if $\langle v | w \rangle = \sum_k \bar{v}_k w_k = 0$.  An orthogonal \emph{triad} is a set of three mutually orthogonal rays (an orthonormal basis up to normalization).  A \emph{KS coloring} assigns each ray a value in $\{0,1\}$ such that (I) orthogonal rays cannot both receive~1, and (II) every triad contains exactly one~1.  A ray set is \emph{KS-uncolorable} if no such coloring exists.

For $\zeta = e^{2\pi i/n}$, the ray set $S_n$ consists of all projectively distinct (modulo $\C^\times$) nonzero vectors with coordinates in $\{0\} \cup \{\zeta^j : 0 \leq j < n\}$.  We classify rays by \emph{zero pattern}: \emph{axis rays} have two zero coordinates (3~rays); \emph{one-zero rays} have exactly one zero ($3n$~rays: normalizing the first nonzero coordinate to~1 leaves one free phase, and there are 3~choices of zero position); \emph{all-nonzero rays} have no zeros ($n^2$~rays: normalizing the first coordinate to~1 leaves two free phases).

A key structural observation: in $\C^3$, the inner product $\langle v | w \rangle = \sum_k \bar{v}_k w_k$ has at most three nonzero terms.  Each nonzero term is $\overline{\zeta^a}\zeta^b = \zeta^{b-a}$, an $n$th root of unity.  Therefore only 1-, 2-, and 3-term vanishing sums of $n$th roots arise as orthogonality conditions in~$S_n$.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}}

\subsection{Sufficiency}

If $6 \mid n$, then $\zeta^{n/6}$ generates the 6th roots of unity as a subgroup of $\langle\zeta\rangle$, so the alphabet for $n = 6$ embeds into that for general~$n$.  Since $S_6$ contains Cabello's 33-vector KS set~\cite{Cabello2025simplest} and KS-uncolorability is monotone (any superset of an uncolorable set is uncolorable), $S_n$ is KS-uncolorable. $\square$

\subsection{Necessity: vanishing-sum analysis}

We show that if $6 \nmid n$, then $S_n$ admits a valid KS coloring.  The proof rests on two lemmas characterizing \emph{short} vanishing sums of roots of unity (2 or 3 terms).  We emphasize that longer vanishing sums of $n$th roots can exist for arbitrary~$n$ (see Lam and Leung~\cite{LamLeung2000} for the general classification), but only sums of $\leq 3$ terms arise as orthogonality conditions in~$\C^3$ (Section~2), so the short-sum characterization suffices.

\begin{lemma}[Three-term vanishing sums]\label{lem:3term}
If $u, v, w \in \langle\zeta\rangle$ and $u + v + w = 0$, then $3 \mid n$ and $\{u, v, w\} = \{\lambda, \lambda\omega, \lambda\omega^2\}$ for some $\lambda \in \langle\zeta\rangle$, where $\omega = \zeta^{n/3}$.  Conversely, $3 \mid n$ suffices.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $u = \zeta^a$, $v = \zeta^b$, $w = \zeta^c$.  If $a = b$, then $\zeta^a(2 + \zeta^{c-a}) = 0$ forces $|\zeta^{c-a}| = 2 \neq 1$; similarly for other coincidences, so $a, b, c$ are distinct.  Dividing by $\zeta^a$: $1 + \zeta^p + \zeta^q = 0$ with $p = b{-}a$, $q = c{-}a$ both nonzero.  Three unit-modulus numbers summing to zero form an equilateral triangle: $|-1 - \zeta^p| = 1$ gives $\cos(2\pi p/n) = -1/2$, hence $p/n \in \{1/3, 2/3\}$, requiring $3 \mid n$.  Then $\{1, \zeta^p, \zeta^q\} = \{1, \omega, \omega^2\}$, confirming the classification $\{u,v,w\} = \{\lambda, \lambda\omega, \lambda\omega^2\}$ with $\lambda = \zeta^a$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Two-term vanishing sums]\label{lem:2term}
The equation $\zeta^a + \zeta^b = 0$ has a solution if and only if $2 \mid n$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\zeta^a + \zeta^b = 0$ iff $\zeta^{b-a} = -1$ iff $-1 \in \langle\zeta\rangle$ iff $2 \mid n$.
\end{proof}

These lemmas, combined with the zero-pattern classification, determine all orthogonalities in~$S_n$.  For each pair of rays, the inner product $\langle v | w \rangle$ is a sum of $t$ roots of unity, where $t$ equals the number of coordinates in which both rays are nonzero:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
Ray pair & $t$ & Orth.\ condition$^*$ \\
\midrule
all-nz vs.\ all-nz & 3 & $3 \mid n$ \\
all-nz vs.\ one-zero & 2 & $2 \mid n$ \\
one-zero vs.\ one-zero (same) & 2 & $2 \mid n$ \\
one-zero vs.\ one-zero (diff.) & 1 & never \\
any vs.\ axis & 0--1 & $v_k = 0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\noindent{\footnotesize $^*$\emph{Necessary} divisibility condition for orthogonality to be possible between rays of the given type; the exact condition for a specific pair also involves phase congruences (e.g., all-nz vs.\ all-nz requires the exponent differences to form an equilateral configuration modulo~$n$).}\smallskip

The ``diff.''\ case has $t = 1$ because the two zero coordinates are distinct, so two of three inner-product terms vanish; the surviving term has modulus~1 and cannot vanish.  The ``any vs.\ axis'' case is a structural zero-pattern constraint, independent of~$n$; the three axis rays form a triad for all~$n$.

We now treat the three cases where $6 \nmid n$.

\subsection{Case 1: $3 \nmid n$, $2 \nmid n$}

Any triad requires three mutually orthogonal rays spanning~$\C^3$.  A non-axis triad must contain at least one ray with fewer than two zero coordinates, hence at least one orthogonal pair whose inner product is a 2-term or 3-term sum of roots.  Since $3 \nmid n$ forbids 3-term vanishing sums and $2 \nmid n$ forbids 2-term vanishing sums, no such pair can be orthogonal.  The sole triad is the axis triad $\{e_1, e_2, e_3\}$, trivially colorable. $\square$

\subsection{Case 2: $3 \nmid n$, $2 \mid n$}

By Lemma~\ref{lem:3term}, no all-nonzero orthogonal pairs exist, so no triad can contain two all-nonzero rays.

\begin{lemma}[No all-nonzero ray in any triad when $3 \nmid n$]\label{lem:noallnonzero}
If $3 \nmid n$, every triad in $S_n$ contains an axis ray.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A triad not containing an axis ray consists of rays each having at most one zero coordinate.  Since no two all-nonzero rays are orthogonal ($3 \nmid n$), at most one ray is all-nonzero; the other two are one-zero.  Two one-zero rays from \emph{different} planes are never orthogonal (Lemma~\ref{lem:planes}), so both one-zero rays $r_1, r_2$ share the same zero coordinate~$k$.  But then $r_1$ and~$r_2$ span a subspace of $\{x \in \C^3 : x_k = 0\}$.  Since $r_1 \perp r_2$, they form an orthogonal basis for this 2-dimensional subspace, and any vector orthogonal to both must lie in its orthogonal complement: the $k$-axis.  The third ray~$v$ is therefore proportional to~$e_k$, contradicting the assumption that $v$ is all-nonzero.  A fortiori, three one-zero rays cannot form a triad (the same plane argument applies to any two, and different planes are excluded by Lemma~\ref{lem:planes}).
\end{proof}

Moreover, axis rays are never orthogonal to all-nonzero rays ($e_k \perp v$ requires $v_k = 0$).  Since every triad contains an axis ray, no all-nonzero ray appears in any triad.  Every triad has the form $\{v_1, v_2, e_k\}$ where $v_1, v_2$ are one-zero rays in the $k$th coordinate plane and $e_k$ is the complementary axis ray.

\begin{lemma}[Perfect matching]\label{lem:matching}
For even~$n$, the orthogonality graph on one-zero rays in each coordinate plane is a perfect matching.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The one-zero rays in the $z = 0$ plane are projectively $(1, \zeta^p, 0)$ for $p \in \{0, \ldots, n-1\}$.  Two such rays $p, q$ are orthogonal iff $1 + \zeta^{q-p} = 0$, i.e., $q - p \equiv n/2 \pmod{n}$.  Each ray has exactly one partner, giving $n/2$ disjoint edges.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Plane independence]\label{lem:planes}
One-zero rays from different coordinate planes are never orthogonal.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $v$ has $v_i = 0$ and $w$ has $w_j = 0$ with $i \neq j$, then $\langle v | w \rangle = \sum_k \bar{v}_k w_k$ has exactly one nonzero term (at the coordinate $k \neq i, j$), which is a product of roots of unity, hence has modulus~1. For example, $\langle(\zeta^a, \zeta^b, 0) | (\zeta^c, 0, \zeta^d)\rangle = \overline{\zeta^a}\zeta^c + 0 + 0 = \zeta^{c-a} \neq 0$.
\end{proof}

We construct an explicit coloring.  Assign $e_1 \mapsto 1$, $e_2, e_3 \mapsto 0$.  All one-zero rays in the $x{=}0$ plane receive~0 (their triads with $e_1$ already have one~1).  In each of the $y{=}0$ and $z{=}0$ planes, the matched pairs (Lemma~\ref{lem:matching}) are independently 2-colored: one~1, one~0 per pair.  All-nonzero rays receive~0.

\emph{Verification of (II)}: each triad $\{v_1, v_2, e_k\}$ has exactly one~1.  For $k = 1$: $e_1 = 1$, both partners~0.  For $k = 2, 3$: axis is~0, exactly one of the matched pair is~1.

\emph{Verification of (I)}: we check that no two rays assigned~1 are orthogonal.  The ray $e_1$ is orthogonal only to rays with $v_1 = 0$ (i.e., $x{=}0$ plane rays), which all received~0.  Rays assigned~1 in the $y{=}0$ plane are orthogonal only to their matched partner (assigned~0), to $e_2$ (assigned~0), and to no ray in the $z{=}0$ plane (Lemma~\ref{lem:planes}).  The same holds symmetrically for $z{=}0$.  All-nonzero rays received~0; since only constraint~(I) applies to them (they appear in no triad by the above), and 0-valued rays cannot violate~(I), the coloring is valid.  $\square$

\subsection{Case 3: $3 \mid n$, $2 \nmid n$}

This is the principal case.  Let $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3} = \zeta^{n/3}$, a primitive cube root of unity.  Three-term cancellations create triads among all-nonzero rays, but we show these triads are pairwise disjoint and the KS constraints decompose.  First, since $2 \nmid n$, no 2-term vanishing sums exist, so no all-nonzero ray is orthogonal to any one-zero ray, and no one-zero ray is orthogonal to another one-zero ray.  The all-nonzero and zero-bearing sectors are therefore completely decoupled.

\begin{lemma}[Projective collapse]\label{lem:collapse}
For any all-nonzero ray $v$ in $S_n$ with $3 \mid n$, there are exactly two projectively distinct all-nonzero rays orthogonal to~$v$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Normalize $v = (1, \zeta^p, \zeta^q)$.  For all-nonzero $w = (1, \zeta^r, \zeta^s)$,
\[
\langle v | w \rangle = 1 + \zeta^{r-p} + \zeta^{s-q}.
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:3term}, vanishing requires the three terms to form $\{\lambda, \lambda\omega, \lambda\omega^2\}$.  Since one term is~1, we need $1 \in \{\lambda, \lambda\omega, \lambda\omega^2\}$, giving $\lambda \in \{1, \omega^2, \omega\}$.  All three choices produce the same unordered set $\{1, \omega, \omega^2\}$; what differs is which element is assigned to which of the three terms $1, \zeta^{r-p}, \zeta^{s-q}$.  Since the first term is fixed at~1, only two assignments remain: $(\zeta^{r-p}, \zeta^{s-q}) = (\omega, \omega^2)$ or $(\omega^2, \omega)$.  This gives exactly two projective solutions:
\begin{align*}
w_+ &= (1,\; \omega\zeta^p,\; \omega^2\zeta^q), \\
w_- &= (1,\; \omega^2\zeta^p,\; \omega\zeta^q).
\end{align*}
(The three $\lambda$-values yield only $w_+$ and $w_-$ up to projective equivalence.)  Since $\omega \neq 1$: $w_+ \not\sim v$, $w_- \not\sim v$, and $w_+ \not\sim w_-$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Triad uniqueness]\label{lem:unique}
$\{v, w_+, w_-\}$ is a triad, it is the unique triad containing~$v$ among all-nonzero rays, and $T_+: v \mapsto w_+$ generates 3-cycles.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Orthogonality: $\langle w_+ | w_- \rangle = 1 + \omega^2 \cdot \omega + \omega \cdot \omega^2 = 1 + \omega + \omega^2 = 0$ (using $\bar\omega = \omega^2$).  Uniqueness: $v$ has exactly two all-nonzero orthogonal neighbors (Lemma~\ref{lem:collapse}).  The map $T_+$ acts as $(1, \zeta^p, \zeta^q) \mapsto (1, \omega\zeta^p, \omega^2\zeta^q)$.  Applying twice: $T_+^2(v) = (1, \omega^2\zeta^p, \omega\zeta^q) = w_-$.  Applying thrice: $T_+^3(v) = (1, \zeta^p, \zeta^q) = v$.  So each orbit has size exactly~3.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[One-zero isolation]\label{lem:onezero}
For odd~$n$ with $3 \mid n$, every one-zero ray has exactly one orthogonal neighbor (the complementary axis ray) and participates in no triad.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $v$ be a one-zero ray with $v_k = 0$.  We show $v$ is orthogonal only to the axis ray~$e_k$.  Clearly $\langle v | e_k \rangle = 0$ since both nonzero coordinates of~$v$ are multiplied by~0.  For the converse, consider any other ray~$w \not\sim e_k$:

\emph{Case (a): $w$ is all-nonzero.}  Then $\langle v | w \rangle$ has exactly two nonzero terms (at the positions where $v$ is nonzero), each a root of unity.  This is a 2-term sum, which vanishes only if $2 \mid n$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:2term}).  Since $n$ is odd, $v \not\perp w$.

\emph{Case (b): $w$ is one-zero with $w_j = 0$, $j = k$ (same plane).}  Then $\langle v | w \rangle$ is again a 2-term sum of roots of unity.  Since $2 \nmid n$, $v \not\perp w$.

\emph{Case (c): $w$ is one-zero with $w_j = 0$, $j \neq k$ (different plane).}  Then exactly one coordinate position has both $v$ and $w$ nonzero, so $\langle v | w \rangle$ is a single root of unity, which cannot vanish.  Hence $v \not\perp w$ (this is Lemma~\ref{lem:planes}).

\emph{Case (d): $w$ is an axis ray $e_j$ with $j \neq k$.}  Then $\langle v | e_j \rangle = \bar{v}_j \neq 0$ since $v_j \neq 0$ (the only zero coordinate of~$v$ is at position~$k$, not~$j$).

Thus $\deg(v) = 1$ in the orthogonality graph, and $v$ cannot participate in any triad (which requires mutual orthogonality of three rays).
\end{proof}

By Lemma~\ref{lem:collapse}, the orthogonality graph on all-nonzero rays is 2-regular.  By Lemma~\ref{lem:unique}, the map $T_+$ generates orbits of size exactly~3, each forming a triangle $\{v, w_+, w_-\}$ with all three edges present.  Since each vertex has degree~2 and both neighbors lie in its own triangle, there are no edges between triangles.  The $n^2$ all-nonzero rays thus partition into $n^2/3$ isolated triads, plus the axis triad: $n^2/3 + 1$ triads total.

The all-nonzero triads are pairwise disjoint: no two share a ray (Lemma~\ref{lem:unique}), and no orthogonality edges connect rays in different all-nonzero triads (by Lemma~\ref{lem:collapse}, each all-nonzero ray has exactly two orthogonal neighbors, both in its own triad).  Since $2 \nmid n$, no all-nonzero ray is orthogonal to any one-zero ray.  Moreover, no all-nonzero ray is orthogonal to any axis ray: $\langle e_k | w \rangle = \bar{w}_k \neq 0$ when all coordinates of~$w$ are nonzero.  By Lemma~\ref{lem:onezero}, one-zero rays have degree~1 (connected only to their complementary axis ray) and participate in no triad.  Note that the axis triad is \emph{not} isolated in the orthogonality graph---each axis ray $e_k$ is orthogonal to all one-zero rays with $v_k = 0$---but these pendant edges impose only constraint~(I), not~(II), since one-zero rays appear in no triad.  The all-nonzero triads, the axis triad, and the pendant one-zero rays are therefore mutually decoupled.

The KS coloring constraints decompose into independent subproblems: assign one~1 per all-nonzero triad independently, assign $e_1 \mapsto 1$ in the axis triad, and assign~0 to all one-zero rays.  Every triad receives exactly one~1.  No two rays assigned~1 are orthogonal: within each all-nonzero triad, exactly one ray is~1 and the other two are~0; the single axis ray assigned~1 ($e_1$) is not orthogonal to any all-nonzero ray; and all one-zero rays received~0.  $\square$

\section{Discussion}

The factorization $6 = 2 \times 3$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:main} reflects two independent algebraic mechanisms, each necessary for KS-uncolorability:

\emph{Phase cancellation} ($3 \mid n$): the identity $1 + \omega + \omega^2 = 0$ creates orthogonal triads among all-nonzero rays.  Without it, no all-nonzero pair is orthogonal, and triads are restricted to zero-coordinate configurations.

\emph{Interlocking} ($2 \mid n$): the element $-1 \in \langle\zeta\rangle$ creates two-term cancellations that link triads together.  Without it, the projective collapse of Lemma~\ref{lem:collapse} ensures every all-nonzero triad is pairwise disjoint and disconnected from every other, making the coloring problem decomposable.

The factorization $6 = 2 \times 3$ is suggestively consistent with---but logically independent of---the ring-theoretic result of Cortez, Morales, and Reyes~\cite{CortezMoralesReyes2022}, who proved that $\Z[1/6]$ is the minimal subring of~$\Q$ over which KS projection matrices can be realized in dimension~3.  Their result concerns realizability of $P_v = vv^\dagger / \|v\|^2$ over subrings, a distinct question from ours about colorability of ray sets generated by a restricted coordinate alphabet.  The two perspectives agree that primes 2 and~3 are both necessary, but the mechanisms differ: their result is about ring invertibility of squared norms, whereas ours is about vanishing sums among roots of unity.  The present proof supplies an explicit constructive complement: projective collapse isolates triads when $2 \nmid n$ (Case~3), and plane matching yields an explicit coloring when $3 \nmid n$ (Case~2).

More broadly, the proof technique---classifying orthogonalities by the length of the underlying vanishing sum, then analyzing the resulting constraint graph---may extend to other algebraic families of KS constructions.  Whether the projective collapse of Lemma~\ref{lem:collapse}, in which group-theoretic symmetry forces orthogonal neighbors to coalesce, occurs in other algebraic settings (e.g., Gaussian or quadratic integer alphabets) remains an open question.
