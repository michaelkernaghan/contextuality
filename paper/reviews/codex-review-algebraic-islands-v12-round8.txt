3. **Independently confirmed claims and the calculations that settle them**

**Section 1.1: normalization is sound, once scoped to real rays.** For any nonzero real vector \(v\) and cube half-width \(L>0\),
\[
P=\frac{L}{\|v\|_\infty}v,\qquad \|v\|_\infty=\max_k|v_k|.
\]
Then \(\max_k|P_k|=L\), so \(P\) lies on the cube. Its opposite represents the same real ray.

There is **no requirement that the original representative already contain \(L\)**. For example, \((1,0,1)\) becomes \((L,0,L)\). “Size” allows the extremal coordinate to be \(-L\). Sphere normalization similarly uses the Euclidean norm. Those factual claims are correct; “keeps simple coordinates simple” is an informal advantage, not preservation of the original coordinate set.

**Section 1.2: the rule and spin-one calculation are correct.** An orthogonal pair in three dimensions has a unique complementary ray. Its absence from the finite set does not remove the pair exclusion. This agrees exactly with §3.

In the Cartesian spin-one representation, for a real unit direction \(n\),
\[
\frac{S_n^2}{\hbar^2}=I-nn^{T}.
\]
For an orthonormal triad \(n_1,n_2,n_3\), the projectors \(n_jn_j^{T}\) are mutually orthogonal, so
\[
[S_{n_i}^2,S_{n_j}^2]=0,\qquad
\sum_j S_{n_j}^2=2\hbar^2 I.
\]
Each squared component has eigenvalues \(0,1\) in the stated units. Thus exactly one outcome is zero. Taking that direction to be colored gives
\[
f(n)=1-\frac{s_n^2}{\hbar^2},
\]
which matches \(f=1\) in the formal coloring definition.

The sentences about predetermined, context-independent values and finite KS contradictions are correct under the quantum constraints just stated. Context dependence is not excluded by this contradiction. The only needed bridge is from real spin directions to general complex-ray projectors.

**Section 1.3: the worked completion example is correct.**
\[
(1,1,2)\cdot(2,0,-1)=2-2=0,
\]
and
\[
(1,1,2)\times(2,0,-1)=(-1,5,-2)=-(1,-5,2).
\]
Also,
\[
(1,-5,2)\cdot(1,1,2)=0,\qquad
(1,-5,2)\cdot(2,0,-1)=0.
\]
The first two triples belong to \(\{0,\pm1,\pm2\}^3\). No nonzero multiple of \((1,-5,2)\) belongs to that Cartesian product: the ratio of its second and first nonzero coordinates has magnitude \(5\), whereas allowable nonzero coordinate ratios have magnitudes only \(1/2,1,2\).

Thus the third **ray lies outside the grid**. That is stronger and more precise than saying its displayed vector is not a grid point.

The two definitions of **completed grid** agree. The completion is iterative closure, not merely one round of adding third rays. Statements about points on a cube must remain restricted to the real picture.

I also inspected the local scans of Peres’s Fig. 7.2 and Plate II. They support the historical cube descriptions and their respective coordinate patterns.

**The counts 13, 49 and generically 145 are correct—but the third cube picture is not.** For a generic sign-symmetric coordinate set with \(m\) nonzero values up to sign, there are
\[
(2m+1)^3-1
\]
nonzero triples. Dividing by sign alone is insufficient: the \(m\) scaled copies of each of the 13 \(\{0,\pm1\}\) directions must also be identified. Generically this gives
\[
\frac{(2m+1)^3-1}{2}-13(m-1).
\]
For \(m=1,2,3\), this is \(13,49,145\).

By contrast, seven prescribed coordinate positions on each face of the outer cube give
\[
\frac{7^3-5^3}{2}=109
\]
surface rays. A concrete missing ray is \([1,2,0]\) for
\[
\mathcal A=\{0,\pm1,\pm2,\pm3\}.
\]
On the cube of half-width \(3\), it meets the surface at
\[
(3/2,3,0),
\]
whose first coordinate is not an allowed face-line position. Exact rational enumeration confirmed **145 total rays, 109 represented by the displayed face intersections, and 36 missing**.

For the two-value family, the exceptional nonzero parameters \(\pm1,\pm i\) agree with the theorem: 13 rays at \(\pm1\), 31 at \(\pm i\), and 49 otherwise. At \(x=0\), there are also 13. “Such as \(x=1\)” is therefore a valid example, but the universal figure captions need the domain restriction.

The counting convention is mathematically usable for missing negatives: for roots of unity it counts \(n\) nonzero sign classes when \(n\) is odd and \(n/2\) when \(n\) is even. It does **not** justify identifying the corresponding grids. In particular, \(S_3\) and \(S_6\) both have three classes under this convention, but their colorabilities differ.

**Section 1.4: both displayed real inner products are correct.**
\[
(x,1,1)\cdot(x,-1,-1)=x^2-2,\qquad
(1,-1,1)\cdot(x,1,-1)=x-2.
\]
For real \(x\), the first vanishes at \(\pm\sqrt2\); the second at \(2\). For complex \(x\), the first Hermitian product is \(|x|^2-2\), so label the displayed calculation as real before writing it.

The “at most three terms” assertion is correct. In the two-value family its general form is
\[
c_0+c_1x+c_2x^{*}+c_3|x|^2.
\]
There are finitely many coefficient choices. On restriction to the real line, this becomes
\[
c_0+(c_1+c_2)x+c_3x^2.
\]
Only after removing identities does the two-root argument apply. With proportionality conditions included, the real family does have finitely many exceptional parameters. The conclusion is sound; the present explanation is not.

**Sections 1.5–1.6: the question and lower-bound exclusions are correctly scoped by the formal definition of grid.** The first rung is colorable. Theorems `thm:twosymbol(iii)` and `thm:threesymbol(iii)` give exactly the advertised exclusions of a sub-31 KS subset for two complex values and three real values, respectively. They do not establish those exclusions for completed grids.

The statement that every finite ray set fits some finite coordinate set is also correct: choose one representative per ray in a common basis and collect their finitely many entries together with zero. Add negatives if desired. This establishes containment in a sign-symmetric grid.

The cited general lower bound of 24 is supported by [Li, Bright and Ganesh](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13319). The distinction between the known 31-ray example and the restricted construction theorem is maintained in [Trandafir and Cabello](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.11640v3).

The closing paragraph’s claims about the fixed 45-entry family and its specialization to Peres’s 33 rays match `thm:circle` and `cor:points`. They do not establish a circle of 33-ray sets on the outer circle. The separate inner-circle family is essential context if the closing sentence is retained.

The Prolegomena is about 2,000 words and occupies roughly four pages before the Introduction. It repeats the motivation, definitions, results and limitations subsequently stated more precisely. Cut the “machine with one dial” transition, most of “The hope,” the claim of impossibility in “What was found,” and the closing discovery narrative. Retain a short cube explanation, the coloring rule, the correct completion example and one precise paragraph defining the objects.

“Coordinate values” is a reasonable replacement for “alphabet” when referring to numbers. “Grid” is serviceable for the projectivized Cartesian product if consistently defined. “Grid point” is the costly replacement: the current text uses it for a face intersection, an actual triple, a symbolic expression and a chosen representative.
